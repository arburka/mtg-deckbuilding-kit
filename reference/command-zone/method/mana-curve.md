## What is a Mana Curve? (1:43)

  - Jimmy calls the curve the most important deckbuilding skill and compares it to timing a surfboard to a wave.
  - **Definition:** the spread of mana costs across the spells in your deck (how many 2s, 3s, 4s, and so on). Archidekt's stats view shows it as a bar chart.
  - It is a separate lens from synergy and card-type counts, and it changes how you judge cards ("is this what I want from my four-drops?").
  - Too many expensive cards clog the deck, and many bad Commander games trace back to a clunky start.
  - **Basic rule:** cheaper cards fit the curve more easily, early and late.
  - **Example, Rachel's Go-Go, Mysterious Mime deck (four-mana commander that clones a creature):**

| Turn | Plan | Curve effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | Two-mana ramp | Many two-drops |
| 3 | Cast Go-Go | Some three-drops for flexibility |
| 4 | Five-drop worth copying | Many five-drops |

  - Spell math matters: four mana can be two two-drops, and a two plus a three equals a five-drop's worth of mana.
  - Includes the Card Kingdom, Ultra Pro, and Patreon segment.

## Why is Mana Curve Important? (9:26)

  - Old Magic adage: the player who spends the most mana usually wins. Unspent mana on turns 1-2 is lost development.
  - The two-mana slot matters more than ever because two-mana creatures are now very strong. Examples: Phelia, Exuberant Shepherd; Magda; Bristly Bill.
  - Jimmy's view: skipping turn two can now be fatal, and turn one may matter just as much in the future.
  - **Example:** Rachel's Keruga-companion deck in a recent Extra Turns budget game couldn't cast anything until turn three (a Cultivate) while opponents had plays on turns one and two.
  - Games ending on turns 6-7 means you need to curve out to cast your big cards at all.
  - A slow start doesn't automatically lose, but it means starting the game a turn or two late.
  - **Deeper dive:** the Extra Turns budget game episode.

## The Trouble with High Drops (37:42)

  - Overloading on expensive cards is a common new-builder mistake.
  - Five- and six-drops in an opening hand are effectively dead cards and often mean a mulligan.
  - Expensive cards are easy to get blown out on. A countered or removed Zendikar Resurgent wastes a whole turn.
  - Splitting seven mana into a four and a three means one counterspell only stops part of your turn. The tradeoff is more exposure to board wipes.
  - Big drops draw the whole table's attention and removal. Example: Karlach, Fury of Avernus.
  - Jimmy cuts from the six-plus slot first, keeping only the cards he truly needs.
  - Dragon decks with cost reduction (e.g. a dragon-discount commander) can treat dragons as four-drops. Decks that cheat them in (Ureni) still need early ramp.

## The Advantage of Low Drops (44:04)

  - More small cards give more ways to use all your mana each turn. Hands of large cards leave one choice and wasted mana.
  - One-drops and cheap activated abilities (Map tokens) soak up leftover mana.
  - Rocks that enter untapped (Arcane Signet, Talismans, Nature's Lore, Thran Dynamo) effectively cost less because they return mana immediately. Worn Powerstone enters tapped.

## Playing the Right Low Drops (47:18)

  - Cheap cards are generally weaker, so a deck of only two-drops can't break parity.
  - Pick low drops for synergy (cheap artifacts in an artifact deck) and quality.
  - Cheap ramp is great early but a weak late draw. Cheap removal is good at any point.
  - A low curve lets you develop and still hold up one or two mana for interaction.
  - Bristly Bill, Magda, and Phelia stay strong late.
  - **Don't count cheap removal as curve.** Eight removal spells among 18 two-drops don't give you a proactive turn two.

## Curve Changes Over Time (1:04:01)

  - Rachel compares averages from the show's decks across three periods (see the core numbers table).
  - Two-drops rose sharply from 2017 to 2022 and kept climbing. Three-drops peaked in 2022 and fell in 2025.
  - One-drops stayed flat, since many strong one-drops are better in one-on-one.
  - Explanation: four-mana commanders dominate, and their decks favor two-mana ramp into a turn-three commander, which squeezes out three-drops.
  - Three-mana commander decks vary more, suggesting there's no standard way to curve them yet. Rachel's Hazezon and Anim Pakal are examples that need follow-up plays.
  - The common pattern: turn 2 ramp, turn 3 impactful spell, turn 4 go off.
  - The right curve depends on each deck. Two four-mana commanders can need very different curves.
  - Includes the Card Kingdom and Ultra Pro segment.

## Closing Guidance

  - Plan the first few turns around your commander's mana value and what it needs to do when it lands.
  - Prioritize proactive two-drops and flexible, scaling cards. Keep high drops to the number your deck can actually support.
  - If a deck feels clunky, lay out its curve, check it in Archidekt, and playtest it.
  - With practice, curve-building becomes intuitive and you can break the rules deliberately.
  - **Follow-up mentioned:** Extra Turns, Game Nights, and the show's previous mana curve episodes.

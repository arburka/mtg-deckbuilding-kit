## Purpose and overview

  - Explains how to build a complete targeted disruption package for a Commander deck: how much to run, which kinds, and how to use it.
  - Uses "disruption" (formerly "interaction") for any effect that destroys, exiles, bounces, counters, goads, or otherwise weakens opponents' spells, on the stack or on the battlefield.
  - Covers color strengths, card and mana exchange, how to evaluate a removal spell, synergy, timing, and deck-specific weaknesses.
  - Mass disruption (board wipes) is mentioned but deliberately left out of scope.
  - Ends with each host's favorite less-common removal spells.

## What is Disruption and Why Do I Need It? (00:01:06)

**What disruption does**

  - Buys time so opponents can't win before your plan comes together.
  - Supports your game plan and holds back opponents' plans.
  - Can be used proactively to open the way for an attack.

**When it's most valuable**

  - Works best when you're behind, which in four-player Commander is most of the time.
  - Aggro decks may run a bit less, but should never run zero.

**Two broad buckets**

| Bucket | Best for | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cheap, efficient, early | Slower decks that need to stall opponents until their engine starts | Fatal Push, Swords to Plowshares, Brotherhood's End (mass) |
| Costlier, multi-target, late | Aggressive decks that set up a board first, then use removal to break through | Fire Covenant |

  - Early snowballing threats to answer quickly: Professional Face-Breaker, Faerie Mastermind.
  - Deeper dive: the deck template episode (Command Zone 658).

## Disruption by Color (00:12:00)

| Color | Strengths | Weaknesses and notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| White | Best overall at removing permanents; has exile; some stack interaction now | Land removal is all or nothing |
| Blue | Counterspells (unique); bounce; putting cards on top of the library; goad | Fewer answers once permanents resolve; timing is hard to learn |
| Black | Creature removal and board wipes; -X/-X kills indestructible creatures | Mostly destroy effects; few, pricey enchantment answers; no artifact answers |
| Red | Small creatures, artifacts, targeted land removal; damage can be split; main goad color | Damage caps limit what it can kill; weak against enchantments (Chaos Warp is the main out) |
| Green | Artifacts, enchantments, most noncreature permanents; good at finding land-removal lands | Weak creature removal (fight spells risk a 2-for-1 if your creature is killed in response); essentially no board wipes |

  - Blue examples: Boomerang and Capsize can bounce lands; putting Coastal Piracy back on top of the library wastes the opponent's turn.
  - Red tip: Josh runs Liquimetal Torque so artifact removal like Abrade can hit anything.
  - Green tends to deal with creatures by having bigger creatures of its own.
  - Color pairs:

      - Black-white has the strongest combined removal.
      - Black-green gold cards (Abrupt Decay, Assassin's Trophy, Tear Asunder) beat Beast Within in those colors.
  - A common deckbuilding mistake is pulling removal from the wrong color, for example Beast Within in a deck that has white or black.
  - Habitual auto-includes the hosts flag: Chaos Warp, Beast Within, Generous Gift, Swords to Plowshares, Path to Exile.
  - Pairing examples:

      - Golgari: take creature removal from black and artifact/enchantment removal from green.
      - Boros: use white for spot removal rather than Lightning Bolt.
      - Gruul: Lightning Bolt becomes a good choice.

## Disruption and Card Advantage (00:24:36)

  - In 1v1 a Doom Blade trades evenly. In multiplayer, the two uninvolved players come out ahead.
  - Better trades:

      - Kill a creature in response to an Aura or fight spell, which answers two cards with one.
      - Remove a key engine piece so several other permanents stop working.
  - Cards that hit one permanent per opponent: Soul Shatter, Unexplained Absence (instant, replaced Grasp of Fate for Rachel).
  - Line between targeted and mass disruption: one permanent from each opponent is still targeted; two or more from each counts as mass.
  - Card advantage example: if an opponent has two payoffs and one card-draw source, killing the draw source turns off all three.

## What Makes a Good Disruption Spell (00:31:49)

**Evaluation criteria**

| Criterion | Question to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Efficiency | How much mana does it cost? |
| Speed | Is it an instant or does it have flash? |
| Flexibility | How many card types can it hit? |
| Impact | Does it hit multiple targets, give extra value, or leave a lasting effect? |
| Permanence | Does it exile, destroy, bounce, or put the card back in the library? |

**Spells by category**

| Category | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Efficient staples | Swords to Plowshares, Deadly Rollick, Nature's Claim | Narrow, but cheap and instant; let you develop while holding mana up |
| Flexible | Beast Within, Generous Gift, Chaos Warp, Soul Partition | Usually 3-mana instants that give the opponent something back; Soul Partition costs 2 and makes recasting cost 2 more |
| High impact | Flame of the Anor, Immoral Bargain, Nowhere to Run | Flame of the Anor (3 mana) can kill something and draw two with a wizard; Immoral Bargain can act as a one-sided wipe; Nowhere to Run removes hexproof and ward on top of its -3/-3 |

  - Decks that draw many cards can run narrow, efficient removal because the hand provides the flexibility. Decks that draw fewer cards need each spell to do more.
  - Players often pick flexible removal because mana cost feels less important while deckbuilding than it does in play.
  - Faster metas call for efficient instants. Slower metas can run costlier multi-target removal.
  - Mana exchange: a 1-mana Swords that removes a 10-drop is 9 mana ahead. A 4-mana spell that removes a 2-drop is behind.
  - Flame of the Anor was played on a recent episode of Extra Turns.

| Lands | Otawara, Soaring City; Maze of Ith; can be found with Sylvan Scrying and reused with Life from the Loam |
| Spells | Abrade, Into the Flood Maw |
| Card draw heavy | Efficient instants |
| Big mana | Higher-cost, high-quality answers such as a planeswalker that removes artifacts and enchantments |
| Damage-focused | Silver bullets for permanents that shut down the strategy |

  - Instant-speed removal pairs well with instant-speed blink: if you don't need the removal, you spend the mana on a blink instead.
  - Dragon's Approach decks barely need disruption.

## When Are You Weakest? (00:50:19)

**Weak early** (control, enchantress, big ramp)

  - Prioritize efficiency over instant speed, since you're unlikely to die in the first four turns.
  - Examples:

      - Cut Down
      - Static Prison: holds a permanent in exile for about 3 turns
      - Pick Your Poison
  - Answering a Ragavan or Sol Ring early has a much bigger effect than answering it later.
  - Small early setbacks add up. Removing a turn-2 Talisman can delay an opponent's commander and change who is ready to make the big play around turns 7-8.
  - Deeper dive: the turn order episode (Command Zone 755).

**Weak late** (aggressive early decks)

  - Prioritize impact over efficiency; sorcery speed is fine.
  - Examples:

      - Phyrexian Purge: 3 life per target
      - Fire Covenant
      - Éowyn, Fearless Knight: exile plus protection from that creature's colors
      - Angel of the Ruins: cycles early, removes two things late

## What Are You Weak Against? (01:01:39)

  - Don't try to answer everything. Fill the gaps your strategy leaves.
  - Go-wide creature decks need fewer combat answers but must handle Ghostly Prison and other noncreature permanents.
  - Look at what your playgroup actually plays and lean into it. Example: run extra enchantment removal against a player whose deck uses Solitary Confinement.
  - Don't overreact to rare losses:

      - Infect: blockers and bounce are enough.
      - Mill: Josh has been milled out about 3 times on camera in roughly 100 games and still runs no anti-mill cards.
  - Adjust spot removal based on what your board wipes already cover. With Vandal Blast, run more creature removal; with creature wipes, run more artifact and enchantment removal.

| Strategy | Good against | Needs answers for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goad | Creatures and attackers | Artifacts and enchantments |
| Tokens | Voltron | Fewer single-target creature answers; Maze of Ith is unnecessary |
| Voltron | Blockers (through evasion) | Lands like Maze of Ith and Glacial Chasm; enchantments; spells like Tibalt's Trickery against counters |
| Mill | n/a | Graveyard decks it feeds (run extra graveyard hate) |

  - Consider how others see you:

      - Josh is often targeted early, so he runs heavy interaction and avoids early setup pieces.
      - Rachel stays under the radar, keeps protection ready for when she's targeted, and avoids high-visibility cards like Smothering Tithe and Rhystic Study.

## When Should You Use Your Disruption? (01:13:21)

  - Wait until the last possible moment, when it's clear the threat is aimed at you, such as once attacks are declared.
  - Taking damage is usually fine; losing cards is worse. Taking 8 damage at 35 life is often better than spending removal.
  - Keep track of how many answers of each type remain in your deck. Example: in mono-black, don't use Feed the Swarm on a creature if the deck has only 2 enchantment answers.
  - Blocking or trading creatures can work as removal and save your spells.
  - The more removal you run, the more freely you can spend it. With only about 5 pieces, each one has to count.
  - Deeper dive: the threat assessment episode (Command Zone 652).

## Why Don't Players Play Enough Disruption? (01:18:50)

  - Players see removal as a chore rather than part of the fun.
  - Interaction gives Magic its strategic depth: play and counterplay, uncertainty, and bluffing.
  - To do your own big play, you often have to slow down theirs first.
  - Slowing an opponent down is enough; you don't have to stop them entirely.
  - Removal is hard to use well, and players avoid the regret of using it at the wrong time. The hosts treat those moments as learning, and Rachel compares it to learning a new Arena draft format.
  - Asking the table how to use your Swords to Plowshares teaches you about opponents' decks.
  - Losses where you made mistakes are more useful than losses you couldn't have prevented.

## Closing guidance

  - A typical deck should run about 12 targeted pieces, adjusted for speed, colors, weaknesses, playgroup, and how others perceive you.
  - Use each color's strengths, and prefer spells that give a little extra (lower cost or an additional effect) over generic flexible ones.
  - Focus on slowing opponents down rather than eliminating every threat.
  - Hold removal until you're sure a threat is aimed at you, and keep track of how many answers you have left.
